How rumors travel through online networks

A. Researcher, B. Scientist

Abstract

We studied how true and false stories spread across a large social network over several years. False stories reached more people, and reached them faster, than true ones in every category we examined.

Introduction

Rumors have always moved quickly, but online networks change both their speed and their reach. Earlier work looked at single events, so it was unclear whether the patterns held in general.
We assembled a dataset of stories that independent fact-checking groups had rated as true or false, and traced every share of each story back to its source.

Results

False stories were about 70 percent more likely to be shared than true ones, and the effect was strongest for stories about politics.
Automated accounts shared true and false stories at similar rates, which suggests that people, not bots, drove most of the difference (1).
However, the study only covers one platform, and the fact-checkers chose which stories to rate, so some kinds of rumors may be missing from the sample.

Discussion

False stories tended to be more novel than true ones, and novelty attracts attention, which may explain why people pass them on more often.
